## 0. Posicionamiento propuesto y catálogo

**Posicionamiento propuesto.** NaNSYSTEMS ayuda a organizaciones con edificios e instalaciones grandes a gastar menos energía y agua, a operar mejor sus sistemas y a resolver sus necesidades de TI y de gestión de proyectos, con la experiencia de Ana Echeverría, ingeniera bilingüe con base en Austin, Texas.

**Por qué este hilo.** Lo más fuerte del CV es la conservación de energía y agua en Austin ISD (2019–2024), apoyada en automatización de edificios (BAS, HVAC, EMS). La consultoría de TI (2015–2019) y la formación en gestión de proyectos (máster y MBA) completan el perfil. Un solo mensaje, "edificios que funcionan mejor", reemplaza los tres posicionamientos actuales.

### [blog_post.content] a1-contenido
**ES**
## Por qué empezar por medir

Cuando una instalación gasta más energía o agua de la que debería, la tentación es comprar equipos nuevos. Casi siempre conviene empezar por otra cosa: saber cuánto se consume, dónde y cuándo. Sin esa línea de base, no hay forma de comprobar si un cambio funcionó.

Esta guía resume un orden de trabajo que sirve tanto para un solo edificio como para un conjunto de instalaciones, como un distrito escolar o una red de sedes.

## Paso 1: reúne los datos que ya tienes

Antes de instalar nada, junta lo que existe:

- Facturas de electricidad, gas y agua de, al menos, el último año.
- Datos de medidores y del sistema de gestión de energía, si lo tienes.
- Superficie y uso de cada edificio: aulas, oficinas, almacenes, cocinas.
- Horarios reales de ocupación, incluidos eventos y temporadas.

### Qué buscar en las facturas

- Cambios bruscos de un mes a otro sin explicación en el uso.
- Cargos que no corresponden a la tarifa o al medidor de la instalación.
- Meses con un consumo parecido en periodos de uso muy distinto, por ejemplo vacaciones y clases.

Si algo no cuadra, conviene revisarlo con la empresa de servicios públicos antes de seguir.

### Compara edificios entre sí

Cuando hay varias instalaciones, dos indicadores ayudan mucho. La intensidad de uso de energía (EUI) relaciona el consumo con la superficie del edificio, y la intensidad de uso de agua (WUI) hace lo mismo con el agua. Con ellas puedes ordenar los edificios de mayor a menor intensidad y decidir por dónde empezar. Herramientas de seguimiento como ENERGY STAR Portfolio Manager sirven para llevar ese registro.

## Paso 2: busca primero lo que casi no cuesta

Los ahorros más rápidos suelen estar en la operación, no en la compra de equipos:

- **Horarios.** Que la climatización, la iluminación y otros sistemas sigan el uso real del edificio y no un horario heredado.
- **Consumos con el edificio vacío.** Un consumo de agua o electricidad sostenido durante la noche o el fin de semana merece una revisión.
- **Equipos con fallas de control.** Un sensor o un actuador averiado puede mantener un equipo trabajando de más sin que nadie lo note.

### Escucha a quienes operan el edificio

El personal de mantenimiento y de operaciones conoce las rarezas de cada instalación. Reunirte con ellos antes de proponer cambios ahorra tiempo y evita errores.

## Paso 3: involucra a las personas

Un edificio no gasta por sí solo: lo usan estudiantes, empleados y visitantes. Las campañas funcionan mejor cuando:

- son concretas: qué hacer y cuándo;
- se explican en el idioma de cada grupo;
- alguien se hace responsable de dar seguimiento.

Capacitar al personal de operaciones y de limpieza, y compartir los resultados de cada edificio, ayuda a que los cambios se mantengan en el tiempo.

## Paso 4: verifica y documenta

Después de cada ajuste, revisa el consumo de las semanas siguientes y compáralo con tu línea de base, teniendo en cuenta el clima y el uso del edificio. Anota qué cambió, cuándo y quién lo decidió. Esa documentación evita repetir errores y facilita pedir subvenciones o reembolsos cuando existan para tu tipo de proyecto.

### Qué indicadores seguir

Con pocos indicadores basta: consumo total, EUI y WUI, costo y, si tu organización lo reporta, emisiones de CO₂. Lo importante es elegirlos al principio y no cambiarlos a mitad del camino.

## Paso 5: planifica las inversiones con visión de largo plazo

Cuando sí toque invertir, compara las opciones por su costo durante toda su vida útil (compra, operación y mantenimiento), no solo por el precio inicial. Un equipo más barato que gasta más cada mes puede resultar la peor decisión.

## Errores frecuentes

- Empezar por el equipo y no por los datos.
- Cambiar varias cosas a la vez y no saber cuál funcionó.
- Dejar fuera de la conversación a quien opera el edificio.
- No documentar los cambios.

## Why start by measuring

When a facility uses more energy or water than it should, the temptation is to buy new equipment. Almost always it is better to start somewhere else: knowing how much is used, where and when. Without that baseline, there is no way to check whether a change worked.

This guide lays out a working order that suits a single building as well as a group of facilities, such as a school district or a network of sites.

## Step 1: gather the data you already have

Before installing anything, collect what exists:

- Electricity, gas and water bills for at least the last year.
- Meter data and data from your energy management system, if you have one.
- Floor area and use of each building: classrooms, offices, warehouses, kitchens.
- Real occupancy schedules, including events and seasons.

### What to look for in the bills

- Sudden changes from one month to the next with no change in use.
- Charges that do not match the facility's rate or meter.
- Months with similar consumption in very different periods of use, such as school breaks and class time.

If something does not add up, it is worth checking with the utility company before going further.

### Compare buildings with each other

When there are several facilities, two indicators help a lot. Energy use intensity (EUI) relates consumption to the building's floor area, and water use intensity (WUI) does the same for water. With them you can rank buildings from highest to lowest intensity and decide where to start. Tracking tools such as ENERGY STAR Portfolio Manager can keep that record.

## Step 2: look first for what costs almost nothing

The quickest savings tend to be in operations, not in buying equipment:

- **Schedules.** Make sure cooling, heating, lighting and other systems follow actual building use and not an inherited schedule.
- **Consumption in an empty building.** Steady water or electricity use overnight or on weekends deserves a look.
- **Equipment with control faults.** A failed sensor or actuator can keep a unit running harder than needed without anyone noticing.

### Listen to the people who run the building

Maintenance and operations staff know each facility's quirks. Meeting with them before proposing changes saves time and avoids mistakes.

## Step 3: involve people

A building does not use resources on its own: students, employees and visitors use it. Campaigns work better when they:

- are specific: what to do and when;
- are explained in each group's language;
- have someone responsible for following up.

Training operations and housekeeping staff, and sharing each building's results, helps the changes last.

## Step 4: verify and document

After each adjustment, review consumption over the following weeks and compare it with your baseline, taking weather and building use into account. Write down what changed, when and who decided it. That record prevents repeating mistakes and makes it easier to apply for grants or rebates when they exist for your type of project.

### Which indicators to follow

A few indicators are enough: total consumption, EUI and WUI, cost and, if your organization reports it, CO₂ emissions. What matters is choosing them at the start and not changing them halfway through.

## Step 5: plan investments with a long-term view

When it is time to invest, compare the options by their cost over their whole useful life (purchase, operation and maintenance), not only by the initial price. A cheaper unit that uses more every month can turn out to be the worst decision.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with the equipment instead of the data.
- Changing several things at once and not knowing which one worked.
- Leaving the people who run the building out of the conversation.
- Not documenting the changes.

## Takeaway

Measure, prioritize, adjust, verify and document: that cycle works just as well for one building as for a school district. If you want help putting it in place in your organization, tell us about your case in the quote form.

## What a building automation system is

A building automation system, or BAS, is the set of controllers, sensors and software that runs a building's climate control and other systems. It does exactly what someone programmed it to do, and buildings change over time: they get remodeled, change use, change schedules. That is why a BAS that is never reviewed ends up working for a building that no longer exists.

These six steps help you start getting more from it without drastic changes.

## Step 1: know what you have

Before touching anything, gather the basics:

- An inventory of the HVAC equipment: type, location, the zone it serves and whether it is connected to the system.
- A list of who has access to the system and who can change schedules.
- The drawings and documentation from the original installation, if they exist.

### A simple inventory is enough

You do not need a complex database. A spreadsheet with one unit per row, its location and its status is a good start, and you can fill it in gradually.

## Step 2: review the schedules

Schedules are the simplest lever and often the one with the most room. Compare what is programmed with real use: shifts, events, weekends, holidays.

### Useful questions

- Are there zones being conditioned when nobody is there?
- Does the schedule change between seasons, or is it the same all year?
- Did someone change a schedule for a one-off need and never set it back?

## Step 3: look at alarms and trends

An alarm that gets ignored stops being useful. Review the list of active alarms: the ones that keep repeating point to a real problem or a badly set threshold. Trend charts, such as a zone's temperature or a unit's status through the day, let you spot odd behavior without going on site.

### How to prioritize

Start with alarms that affect comfort or people's safety, continue with the ones that repeat every day and leave for last those with no clear consequences.

## Step 4: diagnose before sending a technician

Many faults can be identified remotely from the system itself: a sensor with impossible readings, an actuator that does not respond, a unit that does not follow the controller's command. With that diagnosis, the technician arrives knowing what to look for and what to bring, and the visit is more productive.

## Step 5: change things gradually and document

Change one thing at a time and watch occupant comfort and consumption for a few weeks. If the result is not what you expected, the record lets you go back without guessing.

### What to write in the log

- Date and affected zone or unit.
- Previous value and new value.
- Reason for the change and who approved it.
- What you expect to see and when you will check.

## Step 6: train the people who operate it

A well-tuned BAS drifts out of tune if nobody understands how it works. A short written procedure and a training session for operations and maintenance staff keep the changes from getting lost when the team changes.

## Common mistakes

- Overriding schedules or alarms "temporarily" and forgetting about it.
- Changing parameters without leaving a record.
- Treating the BAS as a maintenance-only topic instead of an operations and energy topic.

## When to ask for help

If you do not know where to start, if the system has problems that keep coming back or if you need to coordinate several parties (operations, controls, purchasing), an outside review can save time. If you want us to review your system with you, request a quote through the form.
